<big><b>Welcome to the AI OS</b></big>

The AI OS is an assistant that does jobs on this computer for you. You tell it what you want in plain words, in the box at the bottom of the chat window. It writes and changes files, installs programs, changes settings, and can work the windows of your programs. It keeps a record of everything it does, so you can read it back and undo it.


<big><b>How to talk to it</b></big>

• <b>Ask a question</b> and it answers.
• <b>Ask for a job</b> ("make me a script that renames my photos by date") and it starts a project. It may ask you one to three questions first, then says what it understood and gets to work.
• <b>Say "you decide"</b> when you don't want questions: it makes the choices itself.
• <b>Tell it something to keep</b> ("always use Python 3", "my projects go under Work") and it remembers it for every job after.
• <b>"stop"</b> stops the job it is working on. So does the red <b>Stop</b> button at the top.
• <b>"undo"</b> puts back what the last job changed, and says in plain words what it put back.

The AI does not remember the chat itself. What is worth keeping it writes down: in each project's notes, and in the things you told it to keep.


<big><b>The cards</b></big>

• <b>Building</b>: a job in progress, with a tick next to each step as it finishes.
• <b>Needs your OK</b> (orange): the AI wants to do something that could hurt, like changing a system file or pressing Delete in a program. Nothing happens until you press Yes.
• <b>A question</b> (blue): type your answer in the box.
• <b>Done</b> (green): the job is finished and proved. The files it changed are listed with an Open button, and Undo is underneath.
• <b>Failed</b> (red): what went wrong, in plain words.

While the AI is working, the circle next to the status line turns.


<big><b>The buttons at the top</b></big>

• <b>Clear</b> empties the chat. A job still running and a question still waiting stay.
• <b>Model</b> lists the AI models it can find on your computer and your network. Press one to switch. Finish or stop a job first.
• <b>Help</b> opens this guide.
• <b>Stop</b> shows up only while a job runs.

When a new version is out, a card at login says so. Press <b>Update now</b>, type your password in the window that opens, then restart the computer.


<big><b>What it can do</b></big>

• <b>Files and projects</b>: write, change and run code and documents in its own work area, <tt>/data</tt>. Each project keeps a short notes file, BLUEPRINT.md, so the AI knows the project the next time.
• <b>Programs</b>: install and remove programs, start and stop services, change settings. Installing needs no OK. It is recorded and can be undone.
• <b>Your windows</b>: open a program and press its buttons, type into it and read it, the way a person would. Say "take my Text Editor window" to hand it a window you have open.
• <b>The screen</b>, as a last resort, for what it can't reach any other way (a web page, for example). It looks at the screen, zooms in and clicks. <b>Move the mouse or type and it stops at once.</b>

Its work runs in a closed area of its own that cannot reach the internet or your personal files. Anything outside that area needs your OK, except reading settings and installing.


<big><b>The AI model: its brain</b></big>

The AI OS needs a model to think with. The model runs in a separate program, the <i>runner</i>, on this computer or on another computer in your home.

<b>Runners it works with:</b>
• <b>Ollama</b> (ollama.com): free, on Windows, Mac or Linux.
• <b>LM Studio</b> (lmstudio.ai): free, on Windows, Mac or Linux. Turn on "Serve on Local Network" in its server settings, and load the model with a context length of 8192 or more. If it asks for an API key, the AI OS asks you for it once.

<b>Which model:</b> one that can read pictures, so it can use the screen. Bonsai, Gemma 4, Ornith and Qwen 3.5 can. A 9B model needs a graphics card with about 8 GB of memory. Without one it runs on the processor and is slow. A bigger model on a stronger computer in your home does better work.

<b>A runner on another computer</b> must accept connections from the network (for Ollama, set OLLAMA_HOST to 0.0.0.0), and that computer's firewall must let them in. The AI OS looks for runners on your home network when it installs, when you press Model, and by itself when the one it uses disappears.

Everything you ask goes to the runner you chose. On your own computers nothing leaves your home.


<big><b>Cloud models (coming soon)</b></big>

Soon the AI OS will also be able to use free cloud models, which are much stronger than what a home computer can run. You will give it one key per provider. When one provider's free allowance runs out, it moves to the next by itself.

Cloud stays <b>off</b> unless you turn it on for a job, because what you ask then leaves your computer. Some free plans use what you send to train their models.

<b>Accounts to create</b> (all free; each gives you one key):
• <b>Ollama</b> (ollama.com): sign in to the Ollama app. Its cloud models have names ending in "-cloud".
• <b>GitHub Models</b> (github.com/marketplace/models): uses a GitHub account.
• <b>Google AI Studio</b> (aistudio.google.com): Gemini models, with a Google account.
• <b>Groq</b> (console.groq.com): very fast.
• <b>OpenRouter</b> (openrouter.ai): many free models behind one account.
• <b>Mistral</b> (console.mistral.ai).
• <b>Cerebras</b> (cloud.cerebras.ai).

You don't need all of them. Start with one or two, and add more later.


<big><b>When something goes wrong</b></big>

Open a Terminal (Ctrl+Alt+T) and type:

<tt>ai-os-check</tt>

<tt>AI OS ready</tt> means everything is in place. Anything else is the first problem it found, in plain words.

The AI's own log, for when a job fails:

<tt>journalctl --user -u ai-os-engine -n 40 --no-pager</tt>
