<big><b>Welcome to the AI OS</b></big>

The AI OS is an assistant that does jobs on this computer for you. You tell it what you want in plain words, in the box at the bottom of the chat window. It writes and changes files, installs programs, changes settings, and can work the windows of your programs.

<b>It has full access to this computer</b>: every folder, the internet, and administrator (root) rights. It never stops to ask your OK, and there is no undo. Before a big job, take a snapshot of the virtual machine in VirtualBox: that is the way back if something goes wrong.


<big><b>How to talk to it</b></big>

• <b>Ask a question</b> and it answers.
• <b>Ask for a job</b> ("make me a script that renames my photos by date") and it starts a project. It may ask you one to three questions first, then says what it understood and gets to work.
• <b>Say "you decide"</b> when you don't want questions: it makes the choices itself.
• <b>Tell it something to keep</b> ("always use Python 3", "my projects go under Work") and it remembers it for every job after.
• <b>"stop"</b> stops the job it is working on. So does the red <b>Stop</b> button at the top. The chat stays, so you can say what you want differently. To start clean instead, press <b>Clear</b> on the Stopped card.

The AI remembers only the last few messages of the chat, and forgets them when you press Clear. What is worth keeping it writes down: in each project's notes, and in the things you told it to keep.


<big><b>The cards</b></big>

• <b>Building</b>: a job in progress, with a tick next to each step as it finishes.
• <b>A question</b> (blue): click one of the answers under it, or type your own in the box. When it asks several questions, your clicks gather in the box and go once each question has one.
• <b>Done</b> (green): the job is finished and proved. The files it changed are listed with an Open button.
• <b>Failed</b> (red): what went wrong, in plain words.

While the AI is working, the circle at the bottom turns and the line beside it says what it is doing right now: which step of how many, and the command or file it is working on — for example <tt>Step 2 of 4: running sudo apt-get install -y blender</tt>. After twenty seconds it also says how long that has been going, so you can tell a long job from a stuck one. When a job ends, it says it is thinking about what to remember; that is the last thing it does, and it can take a few minutes on a slow model.


<big><b>The buttons at the top</b></big>

• <b>Clear</b> empties the chat and the AI forgets it: a clean start. Your projects, skills and the things you told it to keep stay; name a project to go back to it. A job still running and a question still waiting stay.
• <b>Model</b> lists the AI models it can find on your computer and your network. Press one to switch. Finish or stop a job first. Your cloud accounts are there too. The same card has a bar, <b>How much it can hold</b>: move it to the context length you loaded the model with and press Set. The AI assumes 8k until you tell it otherwise, and on a bigger model that means it reads less of a window than it could.
• <b>Cloud</b> turns your cloud accounts on and off (see Cloud models below).
• <b>Skills</b> shows what the AI has learned, and a way to delete it (see Skills below).
• <b>Help</b> opens this guide.
• <b>Stop</b> shows up only while a job runs.

When a new version is out, a card at login says so. Press <b>Update now</b>, type your password in the window that opens, then restart the computer.


<big><b>What it can do</b></big>

• <b>Files and projects</b>: write, change and run code and documents anywhere. Projects live in <tt>/data/projects</tt>. Each project keeps a short notes file, BLUEPRINT.md, so the AI knows the project the next time.
• <b>Programs</b>: install and remove programs, start and stop services, change settings.
• <b>Your windows</b>: open a program and press its buttons, type into it and read it, the way a person would. Say "take my Text Editor window" to hand it a window you have open.
• <b>The screen</b>, as a last resort, for what it can't reach any other way (a web page, for example, or a program that draws its own buttons and tells the computer nothing about them, like Blender or a game). It switches to the screen by itself when it asks for a window it cannot read, so it never reports a program missing while you are looking at it. It looks at the screen, zooms in and clicks, using the real mouse. It keeps going if you move the mouse too, so leave it alone while it clicks, or the two of you pull the pointer from each other.


<big><b>Skills: what it learns</b></big>

After a job that worked, the AI writes down what it would want to know straight away next time. It keeps these in notebooks:
• <b>This computer</b>: how your computer and its programs are used, like how to open a website. It reads this before every job.
• <b>A notebook per craft</b>, like "blender" or "web design": ways of doing things that worked, mistakes and how they were fixed, and what you liked. It reads one when a job is in that area.

The Done card says what it learned ("Learned: open a website"). If it learned something that changes the computer, like installing a program, it asks first and shows what it would keep and the steps that did it: press <b>Keep</b> or <b>Discard</b>. Only the newest one waits: if you answer neither, the next job drops it.
Press <b>Skills</b> at the top to read the notebooks and delete anything with ✕. A tip that stopped working is marked, and the AI fixes or removes it the next time.


<big><b>The AI model: its brain</b></big>

The AI OS needs a model to think with. The model runs in a separate program, the <i>runner</i>, on this computer or on another computer in your home.

<b>Runners it works with:</b>
• <b>Ollama</b> (ollama.com): free, on Windows, Mac or Linux.
• <b>LM Studio</b> (lmstudio.ai): free, on Windows, Mac or Linux. Turn on "Serve on Local Network" in its server settings, and load the model with a context length of 8192 or more — whatever you choose, move the <b>How much it can hold</b> bar on the Model card to the same number, or the AI keeps working as if it were 8k. If it asks for an API key, the AI OS asks you for it once.

<b>Which model:</b> one that can read pictures, so it can use the screen. Bonsai, Gemma 4, Ornith and Qwen 3.5 can. A 9B model needs a graphics card with about 8 GB of memory. Without one it runs on the processor and is slow. A bigger model on a stronger computer in your home does better work.

<b>A runner on another computer</b> must accept connections from the network (for Ollama, set OLLAMA_HOST to 0.0.0.0), and that computer's firewall must let them in. The AI OS looks for runners on your home network when it installs, when you press Model, and by itself when the one it uses disappears.

Everything you ask goes to the runner you chose. On your own computers nothing leaves your home.


<big><b>Cloud models</b></big>

Cloud models are much stronger than what a home computer can run, and several providers give some use for free. The AI OS can use your cloud accounts, one key each. When one account's free allowance runs out, the next takes over by itself. When all of them have run out, your own model answers again.

<b>The Cloud button</b> at the top turns this on and off, and it stays the way you left it. While it is on, what you ask leaves your computer. Some free plans use what you send to train their models.

<b>To add an account:</b> press <b>Model</b>, then <b>Cloud accounts…</b>, paste the key, and press the provider's button. Pick a model from the list it shows, and Cloud turns on. The same card lists your accounts in the order they are tried, each with a Remove button.
• <b>NVIDIA</b> (build.nvidia.com): sign in, then <b>Get API Key</b>. It gives free credits for many large models.
• <b>OpenRouter</b> (openrouter.ai, then Keys): many models behind one key. Only its free models are listed (their names end in ":free"); they allow a limited number of answers a day.
• <b>Ollama</b> (ollama.com, Settings, then Keys): its cloud models may need a paid plan.

<b>More providers are coming</b> (all free; each gives you one key):
• <b>GitHub Models</b> (github.com/marketplace/models): uses a GitHub account.
• <b>Google AI Studio</b> (aistudio.google.com): Gemini models, with a Google account.
• <b>Groq</b> (console.groq.com): very fast.
• <b>Mistral</b> (console.mistral.ai).
• <b>Cerebras</b> (cloud.cerebras.ai).


<big><b>When something goes wrong</b></big>

Open a Terminal (Ctrl+Alt+T) and type:

<tt>ai-os-check</tt>

<tt>AI OS ready</tt> means everything is in place. Anything else is the first problem it found, in plain words.

The AI's own log, for when a job fails:

<tt>journalctl --user -u ai-os-engine -n 40 --no-pager</tt>
