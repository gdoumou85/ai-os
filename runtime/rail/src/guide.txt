<big><b>Welcome to the AI OS</b></big>

The AI OS is an assistant that gets things done on this computer for you. You tell it what you want in plain words, in the box at the bottom of the chat window. It writes and changes files, installs programs, changes settings, and can work the windows of your programs.

<b>It has full access to this computer</b>: every folder, the internet, and administrator (root) rights. It never stops to ask your OK, and there is no undo. Before something big, take a snapshot of the virtual machine in VirtualBox: that is the way back if something goes wrong.


<big><b>How to talk to it</b></big>

• <b>Say anything</b> — a question, or something to get done ("make me a script that renames my photos by date") — and it decides for itself what to do: answer you straight away, ask you something first if it needs to, or get to work. There are no separate kinds of request to learn; it works it out as it goes. It asks only about what you want, never how to do it, and once you answer it gets on with the work.
• <b>Say "you decide"</b> when you don't want questions: it makes the choices itself.
• <b>Tell it something to keep</b> ("always use Python 3", "from now on, my projects go under Work") and it remembers it from then on, until you press Clear. It only keeps what you say is to hold (words like always, never, from now on, remember); it does not note things down on its own.
• <b>While it works</b>, a card shows its to-do list, when it is keeping one, and a tick or cross for each thing it has done. You can keep typing — what you say joins the work at its next step, no need to wait for it to finish. Say <b>"stop"</b>, or press the red <b>Stop</b> button at the top, and it stops right there. The chat stays, so you can say what you want differently. To start clean instead, press <b>Clear</b>.

The AI keeps the whole chat since your last Clear, as far as it has room for — how much that is depends on the model's <b>How much it can hold</b> setting (see Model, below); when there is more to the chat than it can hold at once, the oldest of it drops away first and the newest stays. It starts a fresh chat by itself when AI OS restarts, and when you name a different project than the one you were talking about ("now the car rental site"), and says so: it works from that project's notes, so two projects never mix. What is worth keeping longer than that it writes down: in each project's notes, and in the things you told it to keep (it shows these as "Noted for the future"). Clear forgets the chat and those notes; the AI cannot forget them itself, so asking it to just points you to Clear. A fresh chat by itself keeps what you told it to keep; only Clear drops that too.

<b>What the AI knows about this computer.</b> Before every answer it looks at what is installed: the programs you can open, the usual tools, and whatever it installed itself. So it uses them rather than installing them again, and you never need to tell it "Blender is already installed". When it does install something, it installs it the standard way so it shows up next time. The list is at the top of the <b>Skills</b> page, under <b>Installed on this computer</b>. Clear does not touch it: it is what is on the computer, not something the AI remembers.


<big><b>The cards</b></big>

• <b>Working</b>: its to-do list, when it is keeping one, and each thing it has done so far with a tick or a cross next to it.
• <b>A question</b> (blue): click one of the answers under it, or type your own in the box. When it asks several questions, your clicks gather in the box and go once each question has one.
• <b>Done</b> (green): finished. The files it changed are listed with an Open button.
• <b>Could not finish</b> (red): what went wrong, in plain words.

While the AI is working, the circle at the bottom turns and the line beside it says what it is doing right now, in a short sentence — for example <tt>installing blender</tt>. While it writes a long answer, like a whole file, the line counts the words as they come (<tt>Writing its answer… 1250 words</tt>); there is no time limit on that, as long as the words keep coming, and <b>Stop</b> cuts it off at once. After twenty seconds on one step it also says how long that step has been going; the word count already shows progress on a long answer. When it finishes, it says it is thinking about what to remember; that is the last thing it does, and it can take a few minutes on a slow model.


<big><b>The menu on the left</b></big>

• <b>Chat</b> is the conversation itself, with <b>Clear</b> and the red <b>Stop</b> button above it. Clear is a clean start: the AI forgets the chat and the things you told it to keep, and stops whatever it is doing or waiting on you to answer. Your projects, their notes, the journal and skills stay; the AI brings a project up only when you name it ("carry on with the car rental site"). Stop shows up only while it is working. Wander off to another page while it works, and the window switches back to Chat by itself the moment the AI says something new, so you never miss it.
• <b>Projects</b> lists every project the AI knows, newest work first. Press a project's name and it puts "let's continue <i>name</i>" in the message box, or press <b>Open folder</b> to open it on the computer.
• <b>Watchers</b> lists what the AI keeps an eye on for you (see Watchers below). A number beside it counts the alerts since you last looked.
• <b>Skills</b> shows what is installed on this computer and what the AI has learned, with a way to delete a lesson (see Skills below).
• <b>Model</b> lists the AI models it can find on your computer and your network. Press one to switch. Finish or stop what it is doing first. The same card has a bar, <b>How much it can hold</b>: move it to the context length you loaded the model with, then press Set, or press a model and it switches with that size. The bar shows the size in use each time you open the card, and an update keeps it. 8k is the least it can run on, but it then remembers only the last few steps of the chat; a model that can hold 16k or more does noticeably better as a full agent, so give it that much when this computer can. With Ollama the model is loaded at that size, so set it only as high as your graphics card's memory allows. <b>Cloud accounts…</b> on the same card is where your cloud accounts live.
• <b>Cloud</b> turns your cloud accounts on and off (see Cloud models below).
• <b>Help</b> opens this guide.

When a new version is out, a card in the chat says so, within about ten minutes. Press <b>Update now</b>, type your password in the window that opens, then restart the computer. If the window says the update did not finish, its last lines say why. To see which version you have, type <tt>cat /usr/local/share/ai-os/VERSION</tt> in a terminal.


<big><b>What it can do</b></big>

• <b>Files and projects</b>: write, change and run code and documents anywhere. A big file it writes in parts of about 150 lines, so each part is saved as it goes and a Stop loses only the part in progress; ask for small steps ("one small step at a time") and it does one, says what is next, and waits for you. Projects live in <tt>/data/projects</tt> unless you say otherwise, and the AI always knows where they are. A project you already have somewhere else joins the list when you say where it is ("my site is in ~/work/site, fix its title"). Each project is a folder with a short notes file, BLUEPRINT.md, also inside a folder that groups several (like <tt>Web Games/Pool Game</tt>): the AI reads it when you name the project, or the first time it opens that folder, and if it changes files there without writing the notes back up, it is reminded to. Each new file it makes in a project is added to the notes at once, under Files, with a few words on what it is for, and when you name a project the AI also sees the list of files in its folder. The notes also name the project's other documents, like its designs, so the next conversation reads those before building on them. And every job, of any kind, adds one line to a journal (<tt>/data/housekeeping/JOURNAL.md</tt>): what you asked, how it ended, and the folders and files it touched. Each thing you say starts clean; the AI reads only the journal lines that share words with it, so "delete the flappy files" finds where Flappy was, and "hi" brings back nothing. Clear leaves the journal, like the projects' notes.
• <b>The web</b>: it can open a page and read it as plain text, and search the web for you, without needing to look at a picture of it — this works even on a model that cannot see.
• <b>Programs</b>: install and remove programs, start and stop services, change settings, and leave one running in the background — a web server, a long download — while it checks on it, or stops it when you ask.
• <b>Your windows</b>: open a program, press its buttons and keyboard shortcuts, type into it, scroll and drag things inside it, and read it, the way a person would. Say "take my Text Editor window" to hand it a window you have open.
• <b>The screen</b>, as a last resort, for what it can't reach any other way (a program that draws its own buttons and tells the computer nothing about them, like a game). A program it can script or run from the command line, like Blender, LibreOffice or GIMP, it works that way instead: it writes a small script and the program does the work, which goes faster and fails less than clicking. It switches to the screen by itself when it asks for a window it cannot read, so it never reports a program missing while you are looking at it. It looks at the screen, zooms in and clicks, using the real mouse. It keeps going if you move the mouse too, so leave it alone while it clicks, or the two of you pull the pointer from each other. Only a model that can see pictures works this way; one that cannot skips the screen and does everything through commands, typing and keyboard shortcuts instead. With Cloud on, the AI does not use the screen either: it works programs with keys, their controls and the command line.


<big><b>Watchers: things it keeps an eye on</b></big>

The AI can keep an eye on things while you do something else, and act when something happens. Ask in plain words: "every morning at 8 tell me the weather", "warn me when a file appears in Downloads", "tell me the time every 2 minutes". It also sets one up by itself when its work needs it, like checking back on a long download. Each watcher carries a note from whoever made it, you or the AI, saying what it is for and what to do, so when it goes off the AI knows what it was about.

There are three kinds: a <b>timer</b> (every so often, or daily at a time), a <b>check</b> (a small command it runs every so often; what the command prints is the news), and a <b>live</b> program that keeps running and speaks up the moment something happens.

When one goes off you get a pop-up on the desktop, even with this window closed, and an orange <b>Alert</b> card in the chat shows what happened, why the watcher was there and what the AI did about it. The AI first finishes what it is doing for you, unless the watcher is urgent: then it pauses your task, deals with the alert and carries on where it was. What you type while it handles an alert waits for your own chat. The same watcher going off again within two minutes is counted, not acted on, and the next alert says how many there were.

The <b>Watchers</b> page lists them all: a green dot is on, red is urgent, grey is paused. Press one to read its note and its last alert, and to <b>Pause</b>, <b>Resume</b> or <b>Delete</b> it. A check that fails three times in a row, or a live program that keeps stopping, pauses itself, and the chat says why. Clear leaves the watchers as they are: ask the AI to stop one, or delete it on this page.


<big><b>Skills: what it learns</b></big>

After work that went well, the AI writes down what it would want to know straight away next time. It keeps these in notebooks:
• <b>This computer</b>: how your computer and its programs are used, like how to open a website. It reads this before everything it does.
• <b>A notebook per craft</b>, like "blender" or "web design": ways of doing things that worked, mistakes and how they were fixed, and what you liked. It reads one when what you asked is in that area.

The Done card says what it learned ("Learned: open a website"). If it learned something that changes the computer, like installing a program, it asks first and shows what it would keep and the steps that did it: press <b>Keep</b> or <b>Discard</b>. Only the newest one waits: if you answer neither, the next thing it does drops it.
Press <b>Skills</b> in the menu to read the notebooks and delete anything with ✕. <b>Installed on this computer</b>, the first list, has no ✕: it shows what is on the computer, and changes when a program is installed or removed. A tip that stopped working is marked, and the AI fixes or removes it the next time.


<big><b>The AI model: its brain</b></big>

The AI OS needs a model to think with. The model runs in a separate program, the <i>runner</i>, on this computer or on another computer in your home.

<b>Runners it works with:</b>
• <b>Ollama</b> (ollama.com): free, on Windows, Mac or Linux.
• <b>LM Studio</b> (lmstudio.ai): free, on Windows, Mac or Linux. Turn on "Serve on Local Network" in its server settings, and load the model with a context length of 8192 or more — whatever you choose, move the <b>How much it can hold</b> bar on the Model card to the same number, or the AI keeps working as if it were 8k. If it asks for an API key, the AI OS asks you for it once.

<b>Which model:</b> one that can read pictures, so it can use the screen. Bonsai, Gemma 4, Ornith and Qwen 3.5 can. A 9B model needs a graphics card with about 8 GB of memory. Without one it runs on the processor and is slow. A bigger model on a stronger computer in your home does better work.

<b>A runner on another computer</b> must accept connections from the network (for Ollama, set OLLAMA_HOST to 0.0.0.0), and that computer's firewall must let them in. The AI OS looks for runners on your home network when it installs, when you press Model, and by itself when the one it uses disappears.

Everything you ask goes to the runner you chose. On your own computers nothing leaves your home.


<big><b>Cloud models</b></big>

Cloud models are much stronger than what a home computer can run, and several providers give some use for free. The AI OS can use your cloud accounts, one key each. When one account's free allowance runs out, the next takes over by itself. When all of them have run out, your own model answers again.

<b>Helpers.</b> While Cloud is on, the AI can hand pieces of a big task to helpers that work at the same time, each with a role (coding, design, reasoning, review, debugging, art) and each on another cloud model, spread over your accounts. Their steps show on the card as <tt>coding · model-name: wrote app.py</tt>. When a helper's model fails, it moves to the next model and the card says so. Helpers use commands, files and the web; the desktop stays with the main AI. Stop stops them all.

<b>The Cloud switch</b> in the menu turns this on and off, and it stays the way you left it. While it is on, what you ask leaves your computer. Some free plans use what you send to train their models.

<b>To add an account:</b> open the <b>Model</b> page, press <b>Cloud accounts…</b>, paste the key, and press the provider's button. Pick a model from the list it shows, and Cloud turns on. The same card lists your accounts in the order they are tried; the first, marked <b>in use</b>, is the one the AI uses, and <b>Use this one</b> on another puts it first. Each has <b>Change model</b> (every model its key opens, to pick another) and <b>Remove</b>. When an account's model fails or its free use runs out, the AI tries up to three other models the same key opens, keeps the first that answers, and says in the chat which one it switched to. Turning Cloud on with no account yet opens Model, where that card waits.
• <b>NVIDIA</b> (<a href="https://build.nvidia.com/settings/api-keys">build.nvidia.com</a>): sign in, then <b>Generate API Key</b>. It gives free credits for many large models.
• <b>OpenRouter</b> (<a href="https://openrouter.ai/settings/keys">openrouter.ai</a>, then Create Key): many models behind one key. Only its free models are listed (their names end in ":free"); they allow a limited number of answers a day.
• <b>Ollama</b> (ollama.com, Settings, then Keys): its cloud models may need a paid plan.

<b>More free providers</b>, each with its own key and its own daily allowance; the more you add, the longer the AI keeps going:
• <b>Groq</b> (<a href="https://console.groq.com/keys">console.groq.com</a>): very fast.
• <b>Mistral</b> (<a href="https://console.mistral.ai/api-keys">console.mistral.ai</a>; choose the free Experiment plan): strong coding and reasoning models.
• <b>Gemini</b> (<a href="https://aistudio.google.com/apikey">aistudio.google.com</a>): Google's models, which can also look at pictures.
• <b>Cerebras</b> (<a href="https://cloud.cerebras.ai/platform">cloud.cerebras.ai</a>): very fast.
Free plans change often: when a key stops working, its account is passed over and the next one takes over.


<big><b>When something goes wrong</b></big>

Open a Terminal (Ctrl+Alt+T) and type:

<tt>ai-os-check</tt>

<tt>AI OS ready</tt> means everything is in place. Anything else is the first problem it found, in plain words.

The AI's own log, for when something fails:

<tt>journalctl --user -u ai-os-engine -n 40 --no-pager</tt>
